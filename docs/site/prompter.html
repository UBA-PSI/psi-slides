The prompter

The cockpit can listen while you talk and, when something needs saying, put a short hint on a strip across the bottom of its copy of the slide. Most of the time the prompter says nothing.

It is new, and it stays off unless you switch it on. Start the lecture with --watch --prompter, and even then nothing listens until you press Shift-S in the cockpit, or the ◌ prompter button in its footer, which does the same. Press it again and it stops listening.

It goes online, and it costs money. It asks a language model over the internet, so the talk needs a network connection and an API key from an OpenRouter account, and each call is charged to that account. Its microphone hears everyone in the room, and they should be told before you start.

What it whispers

It speaks up when you are behind time, when a point stays abstract although your slides have an example for it, when what you just said contradicts your own slide, when you are speaking too fast, or when your notes planned something for this slide and you have walked past it. psi-slides counts the tempo itself and gives the model the figures: words a minute, filler sounds, the longest pause.

The slide says 90 milliseconds, and the speaker has just said “I think it was 250 milliseconds”. The red strip carries the prompter’s answer, word for word as the model gave it. It is red because the model marked it urgent, a level kept for something that would mislead the room if it stood.

A hint stays in the cockpit. It stands on the cockpit’s copy of the slide, where you are looking anyway, and Esc takes it off the strip. Nothing the prompter says appears on the projection.

It can add a cue card to a slide still to come, the last slide included, when you said something in passing that is worth saying again there. The cards are the ones K shows, described on In the room.

The line under the strip shows, for the first minute, the words the speech recognition picks up. After that it says when the prompter last asked the model, so you can tell a prompter with nothing to say from one that has stopped.

What it needs

Chrome and --watch. The listening is done by Chrome’s own speech recognition. The --watch flag keeps node build.js running and rebuilds the lecture whenever you save; the cockpit hands what it hears to that running program, and the program asks a language model.

The internet and an OpenRouter key. The model is reached through openrouter.ai. You create an API key in an OpenRouter account, which is also where the calls are paid for, and put it in the environment variable OPENROUTER_API_KEY before you start. Without a key the prompter starts switched off and only writes down what it heard.

Money. What a talk costs depends on the model and on how often the prompter asks. For a sense of scale: one keynote, together with every rehearsal and test run before it, came to about one US dollar with the default model, Claude Sonnet 5. The prompter makes at most 360 calls an hour unless you set a lower limit.

A rehearsal needs no key

--prompter-dry-run does everything except call the model: it listens, keeps the clock and applies the rules on when a hint may appear. Nothing goes to openrouter.ai; Chrome’s speech recognition works as it does in the talk.

The prompter: block in the settings at the top of source.md sets the model, how much new speech it waits for before asking again, how long it stays quiet after a hint, whether it may add cue cards, and how many calls it may make in an hour.

# in the settings at the top of source.md
# duration: the planned length in minutes, for the clock;
# cooldown: the seconds of quiet after each hint
duration: 45
prompter:
  cooldown: 60

# the talk, with OPENROUTER_API_KEY set in the environment
node build.js lectures/my-lecture/source.md --watch --prompter

# a rehearsal: nothing goes to the model, no key needed
node build.js lectures/my-lecture/source.md --watch --prompter --prompter-dry-run

What leaves your computer, and what stays on it

The prompter sends text to openrouter.ai: the transcript and the lecture’s text, speaker notes included. From there it goes to the company that runs the model. The prompter sends no audio, but the speech recognition is Chrome’s, and Chrome sends the audio to Google unless it can run the recognition on your computer; the cockpit tells you which. None of this appears on the projection or is written into source.md. node build.js reads the key from the environment and never writes it into the HTML files.

Each run keeps a log beside source.md, in prompter-<date>.jsonl: what the prompter heard, which hints it showed, and what it wanted to say but its rules stopped, with the rule that stopped it. Beside it, prompter-<hash>.prompt.txt holds the lecture’s text exactly as the model received it. The log holds your spoken words verbatim, so keep it out of repositories you publish. During the talk, Shift-click the prompter button to see the last ten hints and cards.

The microphone hears the whole room

Questions and remarks from the audience are transcribed and sent to the model like your own words. Tell the audience before the talk that the prompter is listening, and switch it off with Shift-S when someone from the room speaks, for example during questions.

Whether you may use it in your setting is for you to check: data protection law (in the EU the GDPR) and the personal rights of the people in the room cover their words too.
